## What we need to establish first

The missile has no thrust, no engine, post-flameout, all the fuel is gone and it's simply gliding through the atmosphere with enough forward momentum that the air can still be used as a lever and all we can control are the fins.

The question the paper answers is: can you steer it to a prespecified line in space using only that lever?

Spoiler alert, the answer is yes.

There are 3 primary things we need to understand before getting into the control design:

1. What forces actually act on the body
2. What "orientation" means precisely
3. Why this is a _path following_ problem rather than a tracking problem

### The only forces in play

The missile is in free flight. Two forces act on it: gravity, and whatever the atmosphere does to it.

Gravity is simple. In the NED (North–East–Down) inertial frame $\mathcal{I}$, gravity is a constant vector $g\, i_z$, pointing straight down. It's the "down" part of the NED frame. It never changes direction nor magnitude and the missile has zero control over it.

For the atmosphere: the missile is moving through air, which means from the missile's own perspective it is sitting still while a wind streams past it, always from the direction of travel, travelling at the same speed as the missile's own velocity (in magnitude) but opposing it(in direction). This relative airflow, $-v_x$ in the paper's notation, is the only source of aerodynamic force.

The aerodynamic force is a single vector generated by the pressure distribution the airflow creates over the body's surface. Let's call it $F_\text{aero}$, we decompose it into two pieces, defined by their direction _relative to the velocity direction_ $v_x$:

$$F_\text{aero} = \underbrace{(\text{component along } v_x)}_{\text{drag}} + \underbrace{(\text{component perpendicular to } v_x)}_{\text{lift}}$$

This is useful because the two pieces do fundamentally different things. Drag is always anti-parallel to $v_x$ so it only ever slows the missile down while lift lives entirely in the plane perpendicular to $v_x$, it curves the trajectory, bends the path and changes direction without (to first order) changing speed.

Also lift isn't always "up", rather lift being "up" is a special case that holds exactly when the missile is flying horizontally and its velocity direction happens to coincide with the horizontal. In full generality, lift is a 2D vector in the plane perpendicular to $v_x$. That plane has a basis $\{v_y, v_z\}$, and the paper's outer loop commands $w_1$ and $w_2$, which are precisely the components of the desired lift in those two directions.

The equations of motion (eq. 1a–1d in the paper) make this explicit. The translational dynamics are:

$$
\dot{r}=v
\tag{1a}
$$

$$
\dot{v} =
g\, i_z +
C_{\text{lift}}(\langle v_x, b_x \rangle)\,
\frac{(v_x \times b_x) \times v_x}
{\|(v_x \times b_x) \times v_x\|}
\tag{1b}
$$

Equation (1a) is kinematics — velocity is the rate of change of position, nothing surprising. Equation (1b) is Newton's second law. The first term is gravity. The second term is lift: its magnitude is set by $C_\text{lift}$, a function of the angle between the nose direction $b_x$ and the velocity direction $v_x$, and its direction is given by the double cross product $(v_x \times b_x) \times v_x$ — a vector perpendicular to $v_x$, lying in the plane spanned by $v_x$ and $b_x$. Drag is absent from this equation because the paper sets it aside as a separate effect: it only changes speed, never direction, and the steering problem only cares about the perpendicular component.

Why does that double cross product give the lift direction? We can expand it with the vector triple product identity:

$(v_x \times b_x) \times v_x = (v_x \cdot v_x)b_x - (v_x \cdot b_x)v_x$

Since $v_x$ is a unit vector, $v_x \cdot v_x = 1$, so we're left with $b_x - (v_x \cdot b_x)v_x$, so $b_x$ with its component along $v_x$ subtracted off. The term $(v_x\cdot b_x)v_x$ is the projection of the nose direction onto the airflow direction: it measures how much of $b_x$ already agrees with $v_x$. Subtracting it strips that agreement away and leaves exactly the perpendicular leftover, sitting in the plane spanned by $v_x$ and $b_x$. That leftover is the lift direction.

This also explains the rolling behaviour for free. Rolling the missile about its own velocity axis spins $b_x$ around $v_x$ in a cone, dragging the whole $v_x$–$b_x$ plane around with it, and the lift vector along with it. But rolling never changes how far $b_x$ leans away from $v_x$, only which compass heading it leans toward. So the lift vector sweeps a full circle in that plane without changing magnitude — the projection being stripped off never changes size, only orientation.

### Orientation is not an angle — it is a rotation matrix

The body carries its own coordinate frame $\mathcal{B}$: $b_x$ points out the nose, $b_y$ to the right side, $b_z$ out the belly. These three axes are rigidly attached to the physical missile. As the missile rotates in space, they rotate with it.

The inertial frame $\mathcal{I}$ is the NED frame: $i_x$ pointing North, $i_y$ East, $i_z$ Down, origin fixed at a point on the Earth's surface. This frame does not move.

The orientation $R$ of the missile is the $3 \times 3$ rotation matrix whose columns are the body frame axes expressed in inertial coordinates. That is, the first column of $R$ is $b_x$ written as $(b_{x,N},\, b_{x,E},\, b_{x,D})$, and so on. It lives in $SO(3)$, the special orthogonal group — the set of all $3 \times 3$ real matrices satisfying $R^\top R = I_{3 \times 3}$ and $\det(R) = +1$.

The rotational dynamics (eq. 1c–1d) are:

$$
\dot{R} = R\hat{\Omega} \tag{1c}
$$

$$
I\dot{\Omega} = I\Omega \times \Omega + D(\Omega) + u \tag{1d}
$$

where $\Omega \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the angular velocity of the body frame expressed in body coordinates, the hat map $\hat{\cdot}$ turns a 3-vector into a $3 \times 3$ skew-symmetric matrix (so $\hat{\Omega} v = \Omega \times v$ for any $v$), $I$ is the missile's inertia matrix, $D(\Omega)$ is a passive rotational drag term satisfying $\langle D(\Omega), \Omega \rangle \leq 0$, and $u \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the applied torque — the only actual control input.

Notice the cascade structure: $b_x$, the first column of $R$, appears in the translational dynamics (1b). The orientation drives the lift force which drives the velocity which drives the position. This is why we need two loops.

### Path following, not trajectory tracking

Most missile control literature is about chasing a target. This paper is about following a geometric path — a specific line segment $\ell$ in the inertial frame, defined by two waypoints $p_0, p_1 \in \mathbb{R}^3$.

The distinction matters for a reason the paper names directly: a gliding missile cannot control its own speed. Trajectory tracking asks the vehicle to be at a specific position at a specific time. If it falls behind, it must speed up to catch the reference point — but there is no thrust to speed up with. The tracking paradigm is simply the wrong framework.

Path following asks only that the center of mass converge onto the line $\ell$ and stay there. _When_ along the line is irrelevant. The collection of all trajectories whose position lies on $\ell$ is called the path following manifold, and the control objective is to make this manifold a stable attractive set — a set stabilization problem, not a point stabilization problem. This is the framework introduced in [Nielsen, Fulford, and Maggiore, 2010] (ref. [21] in the paper) and adapted here.

## The architecture: two loops, two time scales

The system (1) has a cascade structure and the paper exploits it directly. The translational dynamics are slow — it takes the missile many tenths of a second to bend its trajectory perceptibly. The rotational dynamics are fast — a torque input can reorient the body in a fraction of that time. Figure 3 in the paper shows the resulting architecture.

The **inner loop** (fast) treats orientation as the thing to control directly. Given a desired orientation $R_d$, it applies torque $u$ to drive $(R, \Omega) \to (R_d, 0)$. It does not know or care about position or path. It just stabilises a desired pointing direction as quickly as possible.

The **outer loop** (slow) treats the translational dynamics as if the inner loop has already done its job — i.e., it designs a control law for the simplified system

$$
\dot{r} = v \qquad
\dot{v} = g\,i_z + w_1 v_y + w_2 v_z
\tag{3}
$$

where $w_1$ and $w_2$ are fictitious direct inputs to the lift plane. It then feeds the resulting $(w_1, w_2)$ into an **orientation extraction** step that works backwards to find the $R_d$ which would produce exactly that lift vector — and hands $R_d$ to the inner loop.

The simplification in (3) is the assumption that the inner loop settles so fast relative to the outer loop that we can treat $R(t) \approx R_d(t)$ at all times. The paper validates this empirically in Figure 5: the inner loop's settling time is around $0.1$ (normalised units), the outer loop's is around $1$ — an order of magnitude separation, enough to treat them as decoupled in design.

## The inner loop: stabilising an orientation

The inner loop solves a clean stabilisation problem: given constant $R_d \in SO(3)$, find $u(R, \Omega)$ such that $(R_d, 0)$ is locally asymptotically stable for the rotational dynamics (1c–1d).

The control law the paper uses is (eq. 5):

$$
u = -k_p \left(
\frac{
(R_d^\top R - R^\top R_d)^\vee
}{
1 + \operatorname{tr}(R^\top R_d)
}
\right)
- k_d I\Omega,
\qquad
k_p, k_d > 0
\tag{5}
$$

The second term $-k_d I\Omega$ is straightforward: it damps out angular velocity, like derivative action in a PD controller. The first term is the orientation error, and its form deserves attention.

The quantity $R^\top R_d$ is the rotation that takes the body from its current orientation to the desired one. If $R = R_d$ exactly, then $R^\top R_d = I_{3 \times 3}$ and the error is zero. The matrix $R_d^\top R - R^\top R_d$ is skew-symmetric — it has the form of $\hat{\omega}$ for some $\omega \in \mathbb{R}^3$ — and the $(\cdot)^\vee$ map extracts that 3-vector. That vector is the orientation error in a coordinate-free sense, expressed in $\mathbb{R}^3$ so it can be added to the torque. The denominator $1 + \text{Tr}(R^\top R_d)$ is a normalisation that comes from representing this on $SO(3)$ rather than using Euler angles or quaternions.

The paper notes that in practice the proportional error is computed in quaternions rather than directly over $SO(3)$ — but the rotation matrix form presented here is equivalent and avoids both the representational singularities of Euler angles and the double-cover problem of quaternions.

To prove stability, the paper constructs a Lyapunov function $V : SO(3) \times \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}$ (eq. 6):

$$
V(R, \Omega)
=
\frac{k_p}{2}\,
\operatorname{tr}\!\left[
(R^\top R_d - I_{3\times3})^\top
(R^\top R_d - I_{3\times3})
\right]
+
(1+\operatorname{tr}(R^\top R_d))
\,
\Omega^\top I\Omega
\tag{6}
$$

The first term measures how far the current orientation is from the desired one — it is zero exactly when $R = R_d$ and positive otherwise. The second term is a weighted kinetic energy of rotation — zero exactly when $\Omega = 0$. So $V$ is a genuine energy-like function: it is non-negative and zero only at the equilibrium $(R_d, 0)$.

The proof in the appendix then shows that $\dot{V} \leq 0$ on a neighbourhood $U$ of the equilibrium, and that the largest invariant set inside $\{\dot{V} = 0\} \cap U$ is exactly the point $(R_d, 0)$. By LaSalle's invariance principle, all trajectories initialised in $U$ converge to $(R_d, 0)$ — local asymptotic stability.

The technical step that makes this work on $SO(3)$ rather than $\mathbb{R}^n$ is Rodrigues' rotation formula (ref. [13] in the paper). Any $M \in SO(3)$ can be written as $M = I + \sin\theta\, \hat{a} + (1-\cos\theta)\, \hat{a}^2$ for some unit axis $a$ and angle $\theta \in (-\pi, \pi]$. The proof uses this to show that the only $R$ satisfying the stationarity condition $(R_d^\top R - R^\top R_d)^\vee = 0$ inside $U$ is $R = R_d$ itself — ruling out the antipodal point $\theta = \pi$ which would also satisfy the algebraic condition but lies outside the region of attraction by construction.

This is the nonlinear analogue of checking that $A - BK$ has eigenvalues in the left half-plane. On a flat space $\mathbb{R}^n$, a quadratic Lyapunov function and linear dynamics make the check algebraic. On $SO(3)$, neither the space nor the dynamics are linear, so we carry a geometric Lyapunov function and prove decrease directly. Same idea, harder geometry.
