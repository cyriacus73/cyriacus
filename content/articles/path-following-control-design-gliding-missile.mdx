## What we need to establish first

The missile has no thrust, no engine, post-flameout, all the fuel is gone and it's simply gliding through the atmosphere with enough forward momentum that the air can still be used as a lever and all we can control are the fins.

The question the paper answers is: can you steer onto a prespecified line in space using only this?

There are 3 main things we need to understand before getting into the control design:

1. What forces act on the body
2. What "orientation" means precisely
3. Why this is a _path following_ problem rather than a tracking problem

### The only forces in play

Two forces are acting on the gliding missile: gravity, and whatever the atmosphere does to it.

Gravity is simple, in the NED inertial frame $\mathcal{I}$, gravity is a constant vector $g\, i_z$, pointing straight down. It never changes and the missile has zero control over it.

For the atmosphere: from the perspective of the missile, its stationaty whilst the wind streams past it opposite to the direction of travel moving at the same speed as the missile's own velocity. This relative airflow, $-v_x$ in the paper's notation, is the only source of aerodynamic force.

We decompose the aerodynamic force into two pieces, defined by their direction _relative to the velocity direction_ $v_x$:

$$F_\text{aero} = \underbrace{(\text{component along } v_x)}_{\text{drag}} + \underbrace{(\text{component perpendicular to } v_x)}_{\text{lift}}$$

This is useful because the two pieces do fundamentally different things. Drag is anti-parallel to $v_x$ so it only ever slows the missile down, while lift lives entirely in the plane perpendicular to $v_x$ so it curves the trajectory, bends the path and changes direction without (to first order) changing speed.

Also lift isn't always "up." Lift being "up" is a special case that holds exactly when the missile is flying horizontally and its velocity direction happens to coincide with the horizontal. In full generality, lift is a 2D vector in the plane perpendicular to $v_x$. That plane has a basis $\{v_y, v_z\}$, and the paper's outer loop commands $w_1$ and $w_2$, which are the components of the desired lift in those two directions.

The equations of motion (eq. 1a–1d in the paper) make this explicit. The translational dynamics are:

$$
\dot{r}=v
\tag{1a}
$$

$$
\dot{v} =
g\, i_z +
C_{\text{lift}}(\langle v_x, b_x \rangle)\,
\frac{(v_x \times b_x) \times v_x}
{\|(v_x \times b_x) \times v_x\|}
\tag{1b}
$$

Equation (1a) is straight-forward. Equation (1b) is Newton's second law: gravity plus lift. Drag doesn't appear explicitly because it acts purely along $-v_x$, it brakes forward speed but doesn't bend the path. Since steering requires perpendicular forces and there's no engine to compensate for braking anyway, the model sets drag aside and works entirely with lift.

The lift direction in (1b) comes from the double cross product $(v_x \times b_x) \times v_x$. Expanding with the vector triple product identity:

$$(v_x \times b_x) \times v_x = (v_x \cdot v_x)\,b_x - (v_x \cdot b_x)\,v_x$$

Since $v_x$ is a unit vector, this simplifies to $b_x - (v_x \cdot b_x)v_x$ — the nose direction $b_x$ with its component along the airflow direction subtracted off. What remains is exactly the part of $b_x$ that sticks out perpendicular to $v_x$. That perpendicular component is the lift direction.

This also explains rolling behaviour. Rolling the missile about $v_x$ spins $b_x$ around the airflow axis. Picture $b_x$ anchored at the origin with its tip tilted away from $v_x$ by the angle of attack: as the body rolls, the tip traces a circle while sweeping out a cone with $v_x$ as its axis. Lift direction is exactly the part of $b_x$ perpendicular to $v_x$, so it rides along the same circle. Rolling never changes how far $b_x$ leans away from $v_x$, the cone's half-angle is fixed, only its orientation spins. The lift vector sweeps a full circle without changing magnitude.

### What orientation actually means

The body carries its own coordinate frame $\mathcal{B}$: $b_x$ points out the nose, $b_y$ to the right side, $b_z$ out the belly. These axes are rigidly attached to the physical missile, as the missile rotates, they rotate with it. The inertial frame $\mathcal{I}$ is the NED frame: $i_x$ North, $i_y$ East, $i_z$ Down, origin fixed to a point on the Earth's surface.

To say precisely how the missile is pointed, we need to compare its own axes to the fixed ground axes and that comparison is exactly the orientation $R$ — a $3 \times 3$ rotation matrix whose columns are the body frame axes written in inertial coordinates. $R$ lives in $SO(3)$, the set of all $3 \times 3$ real matrices satisfying $R^\top R = I_{3\times3}$ and $\det(R) = +1$. Saying that $R \in SO(3)$ is a short way of saying "this matrix encodes a legitimate rotation."

The rotational dynamics are:

$$
\dot{R} = R\hat{\Omega} \tag{1c}
$$

$$
I\dot{\Omega} = I\Omega \times \Omega + D(\Omega) + u \tag{1d}
$$

Each term has a concrete physical job, so let me go through them.

$\Omega$ is the angular velocity expressed in body coordinates — the missile's spin rate as seen from its own axes. We express it in the body's coordinates rather than the ground frame because measuring from a stationary ground frame would mean the rotation axis constantly shifts as the missile maneuvers, making the equations unmanageable.

The hat map $\hat{(\cdot)}$ converts a 3-vector into a $3 \times 3$ skew-symmetric matrix. (1c) needs to combine $\Omega$ with $R$, and we can't directly multiply a matrix by a cross product. The hat map repackages "cross with $\Omega$" as an ordinary matrix multiplication, satisfying $\hat{\Omega}v = \Omega \times v$ for any vector $v$. The skew-symmetry condition $A^\top = -A$ forces the diagonal to be zero and the off-diagonal entries to come in mirrored, sign-flipped pairs — these leave exactly three independent numbers, the same as the vector it came from.

$I$ is the inertia matrix: how hard the missile's mass is to spin about each of its own axes. The shape makes it easy to roll about the long axis and much harder to tumble end-over-end. Multiplying by $\Omega$ gives $I\Omega$, the angular momentum.

$I\dot{\Omega}$ is the rate of change of angular momentum, the rotational analogue of $F = ma$.

The term $I\Omega \times \Omega$(Gyroscopic Torque) is the missile's own mass distribution pushing against its own rotation. It appears because (1d) is written in the rotating body frame — even with zero applied torque, angular momentum can appear to change when measured from a spinning reference frame.

$D(\Omega)$ is rotational drag: aerodynamic friction opposing the spin. It is the air pushing aganist the body and it only ever brakes the spin.

$u$ is the applied torque from deflecting the fins. It's the only control input in the rotational dynamics.

The pipeline is: orientation drives lift, lift drives velocity, velocity drives position. This is the entire reason we use two loops instead of one.

### Path following, not trajectory tracking

This paper (unlike most focused on chasing a target) is about following a geometric path instead — a specific line segment $\ell$ in the inertial frame, defined by two waypoints $p_0, p_1 \in \mathbb{R}^3$.

This matters because a gliding missile cannot control its own speed.Standard control approaches often treat the target as a 'time-parameterized' trajectory, where the controller demands the vehicle reach specific coordinates at exact moments in time.

Path following is concerned with the center of mass converging onto the line $\ell$ and staying there. _When_ along the line is irrelevant. The collection of all trajectories whose position lies on $\ell$ is called the _path following manifold_, and the control objective is to make this manifold a stable, attractive set. Here, the 'trajectories' are simply different time-indexed realizations of the missile's state $(p(t), v(t))$. The controller does not care _when_ the missile occupies a point on the line $\ell$, only that the center of mass eventually converges to the line and stays there. This is a set stabilization problem, not a point stabilization problem.

## Two loops, two time scales

The system has a cascade structure and the paper leans on it directly. Orientation changes fast: a torque can reorient the body in a fraction of a second. Position changes slow: it takes much longer for that reorientation to visibly bend the flight path.

The _inner loop_ (fast) treats orientation as the thing to control directly: given a desired orientation $R_d$, it applies torque $u$ to drive $(R, \Omega) \to (R_d, 0)$. It doesn't know or care about position.

The _outer loop_ (slow) handles position. It designs a control law for the simplified system:

$$
\dot{r} = v \qquad \dot{v} = g\,i_z + w_1 v_y + w_2 v_z \tag{3}
$$

The outer loop assumes the missile has direct side-thrusters, treating lift components $w_1$ and $w_2$ as free inputs to simplify path-following math. A subsequent _orientation extraction_ step then maps these demands to a physical target orientation $R_d$, which if achieved, produces exactly that sideways push through lift. $w_1$ and $w_2$ are temporary; by the time anything is commanded to the fins, it's gone.

This separation only works if the inner loop settles well before the outer loop has meaningfully progressed. The paper validates this in Figure 5: inner loop settling time around $0.1$ (normalised units), outer loop around $1$. An order of magnitude is enough to treat them as decoupled.

## The inner loop: stabilising an orientation

The inner loop's job is singular: given a desired orientation $R_d$, apply torque so the missile's actual orientation $R$ catches up to it and any leftover spin dies out, i.e. $(R, \Omega) \to (R_d, 0)$.

_Why not just use Euler angles?_. Euler angles suffer from gimbal lock: at certain orientations, two rotation axes align and a degree of freedom disappears from the math even though the physical missile still has it. Quaternions avoid this, but have their own problem: every orientation corresponds to two quaternions $q$ and $-q$ representing the same physical rotation, which can cause a controller to unwind — rotating 359 degrees to fix a 1-degree error. Working directly on $SO(3)$ sidesteps both. The cost though is that this requires matrix operations rather than simple subtraction.

### The control law

$$
u = -k_p \left(\frac{(R_d^\top R - R^\top R_d)^\vee}{\sqrt{1 + \operatorname{tr}(R^\top R_d)}}\right) - k_d I\Omega,\qquad k_p, k_d > 0 \tag{5}
$$

This is essentially a PD controller written for rotations.

The second term, $-k_d I\Omega$, is the derivative part: it's proportional to current angular velocity and opposes it directly.

The first term is the proportional part and is the orientation error. $R^\top R_d$ is the rotation that takes the body's current orientation to the desired one. WWhen $R = R_d$ exactly, $R^\top R_d$ becomes the identity matrix and there's no error. When there's a mismatch, the numerator isolates the antisymmetric part of that mismatch via $R_d^\top R - R^\top R_d$, and the $(\cdot)^\vee$ map converts the resulting skew-symmetric matrix back into a 3-vector: an axis pointing in the direction the missile needs to rotate to close the gap, scaled by how far off it is.

The denominator $\sqrt{1 + \operatorname{tr}(R^\top R_d)}$ is a normalisation that rescales the proportional term as a function of how aligned $R$ and $R_d$ already are. Exactly why this exaxctly, I'm not sure but what it does is keep the proportional term well-behaved across the full range of misalignments, including the awkward case where the missile is nearly 180 degrees from where it should be pointing.

### Why it works

To prove this control law actually drives $(R, \Omega)$ to $(R_d, 0)$, the paper constructs a Lyapunov function: a scalar that behaves like the total energy for the system:

$$
V(R, \Omega) = \frac{k_p}{2}\,\operatorname{tr}\!\left[(R^\top R_d - I_{3\times3})^\top(R^\top R_d - I_{3\times3})\right] + \sqrt{1+\operatorname{tr}(R^\top R_d)}\;\Omega^\top I\Omega \tag{6}
$$

The first term measures how far $R$ is from $R_d$. When $R = R_d$, it's zero else it's positive and it acts as potential energy. The second term is rotational kinetic energy, $\Omega^\top I \Omega$, scaled by the same square-root factor from the denominator of (5), zero exactly when $\Omega = 0$.

By applying the control law (Equation 5) to the rotational dynamics, the authors mathematically guarantee that the time-derivative of the energy, $\dot{V}$, is strictly negative (or zero). This shows that the sustem doesn't blow up but it doesn't yet prove it actually reaches $(R_d, 0)$.

The paper applies LaSalle's invariance principle: find the largest set inside $\{\dot{V} = 0\}$ that is invariant under the dynamics, and show it contains only the target. On $\{\dot{V} = 0\}$, it follows that $\Omega = 0$, which means $\dot{\Omega} = 0$ as well. Substituting back into (1d) forces the orientation error term in (5) to zero.

Because $SO(3)$ is a curved space, there's a risk the missile could get "stuck" balancing precisely 180 degrees away from the target (like a pendulum balanced upside down). To prove this won't happen, the authors use Rodrigues' rotation formula to show that the only place where the energy stops draining entirely (the only invariant set where $\dot{V} = 0$) is exactly at the target state $(R_d, 0)$. This guarantees the missile will asymptotically lock onto its assigned orientation.

## The outer loop: from position error to demanded lift

The outer loop's job is to turn a spatial position error into the lift $(w_1, w_2)$ in the plane perpendicular to the missile's velocity. Finally, the orientation extraction step converts these virtual inputs into the $R_d$ the inner loop tracks.

### A frame that rides the path

Why not just measure the error using standard fixed Earth coordinates (North-East-Down)? Because NED mixes together two distinct concepts: how far _along_ the line the missile has traveled (which we don't care about), and how far _off to the side_ it is (which is the only thing we actually want to fix).

To cleanly separate these, the paper builds a moving coordinate system, the **Frenet–Serret frame** ($\mathcal{S}$), that effectively rides along the target path, always anchoring itself to the point on the line closest to the missile.

The path itself is parameterised as a unit-speed curve:

$$
\gamma(s) = \frac{p_1 - p_0}{\|p_1 - p_0\|}\,s + p_0, \qquad s \in [0, \|p_1-p_0\|]
\tag{7}
$$

Walk a physical distance $s$ along the unit vector from the start $p_0$ toward the end $p_1$, and you land at $\gamma(s)$. Given the missile's current position $r$, the distance along the line to the closest point is:

$$
s^* := \frac{1}{\|p_1-p_0\|}\,\langle p_1 - p_0,\, r - p_0 \rangle
\tag{8}
$$

This is the standard projection formula. If you drop a perpendicular line from the missile down to the path, $s^*$ is exactly where that perpendicular hits. The moving frame $\mathcal{S}$ is anchored right there at $\gamma(s^*)$. Its primary axis $s_x$ points straight down the line, while $s_y$ and $s_z$ define the cross-section (the plane perpendicular to the path).

### The Error Coordinates: How far and how fast

With this moving frame attached to the path, we can translate the missile's messy 3D flight dynamics into six highly intuitive numbers:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\eta_1 &= \langle s_x,\, r-p_0\rangle, &
\eta_2 &= \langle s_x,\, v\rangle, \\[4pt]
\xi_1 &= \langle s_y,\, r-\gamma(\eta_1)\rangle, &
\xi_2 &= \langle s_z,\, r-\gamma(\eta_1)\rangle, \\[4pt]
\xi_3 &= \langle s_y,\, v\rangle, &
\xi_4 &= \langle s_z,\, v\rangle
\end{aligned}
\tag{9}
$$

To simplify things for the controller, these are grouped into two distinct error vectors:

$$
e_1 := \binom{\xi_1}{\xi_2}, \qquad e_2 := \binom{\xi_3}{\xi_4}
\tag{10}
$$

Here is what these states actually represent in the physical world:

- **$\eta_1$:** Exactly $s^*$ under another name. It is **how far** you are along the path.
- **$\eta_2$:** The rate of change of $\eta_1$. It is **how fast** you are moving down the path. Because this is a path following problem (not trajectory tracking), the controller completely ignores these two variables.
- **$e_1$ (The Position Error):** This is your cross-track error. It measures exactly **how far off-center** the missile is from the path in the $y$ and $z$ directions.
- **$e_2$ (The Velocity Error):** This is your drift. It measures **how fast you are sliding** away from (or towards) the center line.

Driving $e_1$ to zero pushes the missile onto the line; driving $e_2$ to zero acts as a damping mechanism to ensure the missile settles perfectly onto the path instead of overshooting and sailing out the other side.

### The Decoupling Matrix and The Singularity

Rewriting the translational dynamics (3) into these new error coordinates gives:

$$
\dot{\eta}_1 = \eta_2, \qquad
\dot{\eta}_2 = g\langle s_x,i_z\rangle + w_1\langle s_x,v_y\rangle + w_2\langle s_x,v_z\rangle
\tag{11a}
$$

$$
\dot{e}_1 = e_2, \qquad
\dot{e}_2 = g\binom{\langle s_y,i_z\rangle}{\langle s_z,i_z\rangle} + F(v)\binom{w_1}{w_2}
\tag{11b}
$$

where the matrix $F(v)$ is defined as:

$$
F(v) := \begin{pmatrix}\langle s_y,v_y\rangle & \langle s_y,v_z\rangle \\ \langle s_z,v_y\rangle & \langle s_z,v_z\rangle\end{pmatrix}
\tag{12}
$$

Look at the error dynamics in (11b): $\dot{e}_1 = e_2$ and $\dot{e}_2 = (\text{gravity}) + F(v)\binom{w_1}{w_2}$. If you strip away the gravity term, this is a classic double integrator — you have a position, a velocity, and an input driving the acceleration.

The only thing separating us from a clean, basic linear control law is **$F(v)$, the decoupling matrix**.
Think of $F(v)$ much like a **Jacobian matrix** in robotics or non-linear control. It acts as a mechanical translator, describing exactly how a change in our virtual lift inputs ($w_1, w_2$) will manifest as actual physical acceleration ($\dot{e}_2$) toward the path, based on the missile's current velocity.

But when can we trust this translator? **Proposition 1** states that $F(v)$ becomes mathematically singular (un-invertible) if and only if $\langle s_x, v_x \rangle = 0$.

- **The Physical Meaning:** This condition means the missile's velocity vector is perfectly perpendicular to the path. In other words, the missile is **hovering sideways** relative to the target line, with zero forward momentum down the pipe.
- Because lift requires forward airflow over the fins, a glider moving purely sideways has no aerodynamic authority to push itself in two independent directions. The math breaks exactly where the physics breaks. As long as the missile maintains a forward trajectory along the path, $F(v)$ remains healthy.

### Section IV-A vs. IV-B: The Ideal vs. The Practical

Before looking at the two control options, it helps to understand what it actually means for $F(v)$ to be **invertible**. In control theory, a matrix is invertible if it provides a perfect, two-way street between cause and effect. If $F(v)$ maps your lift ($w_1, w_2$) to your missile's acceleration, $F(v)^{-1}$ maps your _desired_ acceleration exactly back to the required lift. As Proposition 1 established, this matrix loses its invertibility (becomes singular) only if the missile is hovering perfectly sideways. In that state, the two control knobs ($w_1$ and $w_2$) can no longer independently control the two error directions.

As long as the missile has forward momentum and $F(v)$ remains invertible, the paper presents two distinct ways to calculate the steering inputs.

#### 1. Exact Feedback Linearization (The Mathematical Ideal)

The most direct approach is to force the system to be linear by calculating that exact inverse:

$$
\binom{w_1}{w_2} = F(v)^{-1}\left(-g\binom{\langle s_y,i_z\rangle}{\langle s_z,i_z\rangle} + K\binom{e_1}{e_2}\right)
\tag{13}
$$

Let's break down the signs and the logic here:

- **The Gravity Term ($-g$):** In the physical world, gravity is a positive force pulling the missile's error downward. The controller uses a negative sign ($-g$) to calculate an exact "upward" force to perfectly cancel gravity out before it even thinks about steering.
- **The Gain Term ($+K$):** To fix the error, the system needs to act like a restoring spring, which requires a negative feedback loop (pulling in the opposite direction of the error). While the equation says $+K$, in control theory, the matrix $K$ is designed to secretly contain negative values to pull the error ($e_1, e_2$) to zero.
- **The Inverse ($F(v)^{-1}$):** By wrapping both of these terms in the inverse, we perfectly cancel out all the messy, curvy, non-linear aerodynamics.

The resulting closed-loop system acts like a flawless textbook linear system. However, this is purely theoretical. In practice, calculating $F(v)^{-1}$ in real-time is computationally expensive and physically brittle. If a temporary gust of wind shifts the velocity vector, the inverse calculation can spike, commanding highly aggressive and jittery fin corrections. If the missile even _approaches_ the singularity, the math tries to divide by near-zero and blows up.

#### 2. Linear Feedback with Frame Compensation (The Engineering Reality)

To avoid the risks of the inverse, Equation (14) swaps $F(v)^{-1}$ for its transpose, $F(v)^\top$, sacrificing mathematical perfection for robustness:

$$
\binom{w_1}{w_2} = -g\binom{\langle i_z,v_y\rangle}{\langle i_z,v_z\rangle} + F(v)^\top\!\left(k_p e_1 + k_d e_2\right), \qquad k_p, k_d < 0
\tag{14}
$$

_(Note: The gravity compensation $-g$ is still here, just moved outside the matrix term. And while the previous equation hid its negative feedback inside the $K$ matrix, here the negative signs are pulled out into the open by explicitly defining $k_p, k_d < 0$. It acts exactly like an ordinary damped spring: $\ddot{e} + |k_d|\dot{e} + |k_p|e = 0$.)_

But why is the transpose a valid stand-in for the inverse? This is a classic robotics technique known as **Jacobian Transpose Control**.

- The **inverse** calculates the mathematical "perfect" steering angle to cancel every aerodynamic cross-coupling. It tries to force the missile down a mathematically straight line, no matter how hard the control surfaces have to work.
- The **transpose** is a rougher, but safer, estimate. In linear algebra, if a matrix maps forces to movements, its transpose maps desired movements into forces that are _directionally correct_. It essentially acts like a ball rolling down a hill: it might not calculate the mathematically straightest path to the bottom, but it guarantees that the control effort is always pushing "downhill" toward zero error.

By using the transpose, the controller avoids division entirely, making it incredibly stable to compute. It leaves some non-linear "disturbances" behind, but the authors prove that these leftover disturbances are _quadratic_. As the missile gets closer to the line—where $e_1$ and $e_2$ approach zero—these quadratic disturbances shrink away significantly faster than the linear terms do.

So, right where it matters most (near the path), this cheaper, smoother, and safer controller behaves almost indistinguishably from the "perfect" one. This numerical stability and physical smoothness are precisely why this transpose method is the one they actually flew on the real missile.

### Orientation extraction

Whichever law produced $w_1, w_2$, there is still a physical gap to close: the missile does not have direct side-thrusters to generate $w_1, w_2$. It only has tail fins. The fins rotate the nose, and the entire body's orientation relative to the oncoming wind is what actually generates the massive lift forces. Orientation extraction is the step that translates the outer loop's demand for lift into a target 3D orientation that the inner loop can actually chase.

We start by placing the real aerodynamic lift equation side-by-side with our demanded lift:

$$
w_1 v_y + w_2 v_z = C_\text{lift}(\langle v_x,b_x\rangle)\,\frac{(v_x\times b_x)\times v_x}{\|(v_x\times b_x)\times v_x\|}
\tag{16}
$$

To figure out how to orient the missile to make this equation true, we write out our _desired_ nose direction in velocity-frame components:

$$
b_x = m_x v_x + m_y v_y + m_z v_z
\tag{17}
$$

_(Note: While this is the physical body axis $b_x$, we are calculating its target state, which is why we map it using our desired components $m_x, m_y, m_z$.)_

By substituting (17) into (16) and matching magnitudes on both sides, we get one equation with one unknown:

$$C_\text{lift}(m_x) = \sqrt{w_1^2 + w_2^2}$$

Because Assumption 3 (back in Section II) guaranteed that $C_\text{lift}$ is strictly decreasing over the relevant range, we can invert this function to solve uniquely for $m_x$.

- **The Physical Meaning:** $m_x$ tells us the _total_ tilt (angle of attack plus sideslip) the nose needs away from the velocity vector to generate the raw magnitude of the demanded lift.

Once $m_x$ is known, we have the required tilt, but not the direction—the nose could be pointing anywhere along a "cone of ambiguity" relative to the wind. Matching the _direction_ of the demanded lift against the real lift formula pins down the remaining coordinates:

$$
\begin{aligned}
m_y = \sqrt{1-m_x^2}\,\cos\big(\arg(w_1+jw_2)\big), &
\qquad m_z = \sqrt{1-m_x^2}\,\sin\big(\arg(w_1+jw_2)\big)
\end{aligned}
\tag{18}
$$

That phase term, $\arg(w_1+jw_2)$, is doing something highly intuitive: it is literally the compass heading of the demanded lift vector in the perpendicular $(v_y,v_z)$ plane. It tells the missile exactly which way around the cone to lean. $m_x$ sets how far the nose leans away from the wind, and this phase angle sets exactly which direction it leans toward to get the lift pointing the right way.

#### Resolving the Roll Ambiguity

This pins down $b_x$ completely—we know exactly where the nose needs to point in 3D space. But $b_x$ alone isn't a full orientation. There is still one degree of freedom left over: the roll about the nose direction itself. You could point the nose perfectly, but the missile could be right-side up, upside down, or sideways.

Rather than giving a universal rule, the paper resolves this with a practical worked example. Suppose the missile has a sensor or antenna on its belly that needs to face the ground. We need to lock the roll so the bottom of the missile ($b_z$) points generally downward.

Writing the target nose vector $b_x = b_1 i_x + b_2 i_y + b_3 i_z$ in standard inertial (Earth) coordinates, the paper uses the global gravity down-vector ($i_z$) and a couple of cross products to build the rest of the frame:

$$b_z := b_x \times (i_z \times b_x)$$

The paper demonstrates that as long as the missile isn't pointed perfectly straight up or down, the projection of this $b_z$ onto $i_z$ is always positive. This mathematically guarantees that the "belly" vector really does point generally toward the ground. From there, the frame completes itself geometrically to find the right wing: $b_y = b_z \times b_x$, enforcing a standard right-handed relation.

#### The Final Handoff

That is the full chain, start to finish:

1. Position error in moving-frame coordinates.
2. Linear feedback law (exact or approximate) calculates demanded lift $(w_1,w_2)$.
3. Orientation extraction converts lift into target nose coordinates $(m_x, m_y, m_z)$ and resolves the roll to find target axes $b_x, b_y, b_z$.
4. Handed directly to the inner loop.

While the paper never explicitly packages these three final vectors into a single "Desired Rotation Matrix" ($R_d$), generating these three orthogonal target axes is mathematically identical. The inner loop takes this complete target frame, compares it to its current gyro readings, and fires the fins to close the gap.

### The Rhythm of Flight (Time-Scale Separation)

The success of this controller relies on a strategy called **time-scale separation**. Think of the missile’s brain as a split-personality system:

- **The Inner Loop (The Reflex):** This loop runs at a blistering speed. Its only job is to ensure the missile is oriented exactly as requested. When an air gust hits, the fins snap into position within $\sim 0.1$ seconds. This is the "reflex" that keeps the vehicle stable.
- **The Outer Loop (The Strategist):** This loop runs much slower—about ten times slower than the inner loop. It focuses on the "big picture" of path following. It doesn't worry about how the fins move; it simply assumes the inner loop will handle the physics and tells it where the nose needs to point next.

**Why this separation is vital:** If the two loops ran at the same speed, the missile would become jittery and erratic. The strategist would be trying to change the flight path while the reflex was still fighting the previous turn, causing the system to fight itself. By keeping the inner loop an order of magnitude faster, the "Strategist" perceives the missile as a perfectly obedient object, while the "Reflex" handles the messy aerodynamic turbulence. As seen in the flight data, this creates a clean, staggered response: the orientation settles almost instantly, allowing the path-following error to smoothly drift toward zero.

### The Glider's Dilemma (Energy and Limits)

The final test results reveal the ultimate constraint of a gliding missile: it is a closed system with a finite budget of energy. It has no engine to add thrust; it only has its initial speed from launch.

Every maneuver the missile makes—every turn to get back on the path—is a purchase made using its airspeed. As shown in the flight tests, when the missile maneuvers to maintain a constant altitude, the control deflections steadily increase. The missile is working harder and harder to stay level because the air flowing over its fins is becoming thinner and slower. It is effectively "paying" for its altitude by shedding its velocity.

**The Stall Limit:** Eventually, this "spending" leads to a stall, where the missile loses enough speed that the air can no longer support its weight, and it stops being a controllable aircraft. By design, this mission is not meant to last forever.

**The Descending Advantage:** The data demonstrates that the controller handles a **descending flight path** much more gracefully than a flat one. By allowing the missile to trade altitude for speed, it remains in a healthy "energy state" throughout the entire flight. Even as the missile loses over 20% of its speed, the control architecture remains robust, keeping the vehicle within meters of the target path until the very end. The system is a master of resource management, proving that even a "falling" object can be steered with mathematical precision if the controller understands the limits of the energy it has left.
