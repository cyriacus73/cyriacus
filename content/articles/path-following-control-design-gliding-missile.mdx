## What we need to establish first

The missile has no thrust, no engine, post-flameout, all the fuel is gone and it's simply gliding through the atmosphere with enough forward momentum that the air can still be used as a lever and all we can control are the fins.

The question the paper answers is: can you steer it to a prespecified line in space using only that lever?

Spoiler alert, the answer is yes.

There are 3 primary things we need to understand before getting into the control design:

1. What forces actually act on the body
2. What "orientation" means precisely
3. Why this is a _path following_ problem rather than a tracking problem

### The only forces in play

The missile is in free flight. Two forces act on it: gravity, and whatever the atmosphere does to it.

Gravity is simple. In the NED (North–East–Down) inertial frame $\mathcal{I}$, gravity is a constant vector $g\, i_z$, pointing straight down. It's the "down" part of the NED frame. It never changes direction nor magnitude and the missile has zero control over it.

For the atmosphere: the missile is moving through air, which means from the missile's own perspective it is sitting still while a wind streams past it, always from the direction of travel, travelling at the same speed as the missile's own velocity (in magnitude) but opposing it in direction. This relative airflow, $-v_x$ in the paper's notation, is the only source of aerodynamic force.

The aerodynamic force is a single vector generated by the pressure distribution the airflow creates over the body's surface. Calling it $F_\text{aero}$, we decompose it into two pieces, defined by their direction _relative to the velocity direction_ $v_x$:

$$F_\text{aero} = \underbrace{(\text{component along } v_x)}_{\text{drag}} + \underbrace{(\text{component perpendicular to } v_x)}_{\text{lift}}$$

This is useful because the two pieces do fundamentally different things. Drag is always anti-parallel to $v_x$ so it only ever slows the missile down, while lift lives entirely in the plane perpendicular to $v_x$ — it curves the trajectory, bends the path and changes direction without (to first order) changing speed.

Also lift isn't always "up." Lift being "up" is a special case that holds exactly when the missile is flying horizontally and its velocity direction happens to coincide with the horizontal. In full generality, lift is a 2D vector in the plane perpendicular to $v_x$. That plane has a basis $\{v_y, v_z\}$, and the paper's outer loop commands $w_1$ and $w_2$, which are precisely the components of the desired lift in those two directions.

The equations of motion (eq. 1a–1d in the paper) make this explicit. The translational dynamics are:

$$
\dot{r}=v
\tag{1a}
$$

$$
\dot{v} =
g\, i_z +
C_{\text{lift}}(\langle v_x, b_x \rangle)\,
\frac{(v_x \times b_x) \times v_x}
{\|(v_x \times b_x) \times v_x\|}
\tag{1b}
$$

Equation (1a) is kinematics — velocity is the rate of change of position, nothing surprising. Equation (1b) is Newton's second law. The first term is gravity. The second term is lift: its magnitude is set by $C_\text{lift}$, a function of the angle between the nose direction $b_x$ and the velocity direction $v_x$, and its direction is given by the double cross product $(v_x \times b_x) \times v_x$ — a vector perpendicular to $v_x$, lying in the plane spanned by $v_x$ and $b_x$. Drag is absent from this equation because the paper sets it aside as a separate effect: it only changes speed, never direction, and the steering problem only cares about the perpendicular component.

Why does that double cross product give the lift direction? We can expand it with the vector triple product identity:

$$(v_x \times b_x) \times v_x = (v_x \cdot v_x)\,b_x - (v_x \cdot b_x)\,v_x$$

Since $v_x$ is a unit vector, $v_x \cdot v_x = 1$, so we're left with $b_x - (v_x \cdot b_x)v_x$ — $b_x$ with its component along $v_x$ subtracted off. The term $(v_x\cdot b_x)v_x$ is the projection of the nose direction onto the airflow direction: it measures how much of $b_x$ already agrees with $v_x$. Subtracting it strips that agreement away and leaves exactly the perpendicular leftover, sitting in the plane spanned by $v_x$ and $b_x$. That leftover is the lift direction.

This also explains the rolling behaviour for free. Rolling the missile about its own velocity axis spins $b_x$ around $v_x$. Picture $b_x$ as a vector anchored at the origin, tip tilted away from $v_x$ by the angle of attack: as the body rolls, that tip traces a circle, while the vector itself — tail fixed, tip circling — sweeps out a cone with $v_x$ as its axis. Lift direction is exactly the part of $b_x$ perpendicular to $v_x$, so it rides along that same circle, the cone's rim. Rolling never changes how far $b_x$ leans away from $v_x$, only which compass heading it leans toward — the cone's half-angle stays fixed, only its orientation spins. So the lift vector sweeps a full circle without changing magnitude: the projection being stripped off never changes size, only direction.

### What orientation actually means

The body carries its own coordinate frame $\mathcal{B}$: $b_x$ points out the nose, $b_y$ to the right side, $b_z$ out the belly. These axes are rigidly attached to the physical missile. As the missile rotates, they rotate with it.

The inertial frame $\mathcal{I}$ is the NED frame: $i_x$ North, $i_y$ East, $i_z$ Down, origin fixed to a point on the Earth's surface. This frame doesn't move.

To say precisely how the missile is pointed, we need to compare its own axes to the fixed ground axes — and that comparison is exactly what the orientation $R$ is. It's the $3 \times 3$ rotation matrix whose columns are the body frame axes written in inertial coordinates. The first column of $R$ is $b_x$ written as $(b_{x,N},\, b_{x,E},\, b_{x,D})$, and so on for $b_y$ and $b_z$. $R$ lives in $SO(3)$, the _special orthogonal group_ — the set of all $3 \times 3$ real matrices satisfying $R^\top R = I_{3\times3}$ and $\det(R) = +1$. Saying $R \in SO(3)$ is just a compact way of saying "this matrix encodes a legitimate rotation and nothing else."

The rotational dynamics are:

$$
\dot{R} = R\hat{\Omega} \tag{1c}
$$

$$
I\dot{\Omega} = I\Omega \times \Omega + D(\Omega) + u \tag{1d}
$$

Each term has a concrete physical job, so let me go through them.

$\Omega$ is the angular velocity in body coordinates — the missile's spin rate, measured from its own point of view, along its own nose, wing, and belly axes. The reason we express it in body coordinates rather than in the ground frame is purely practical: measured from the stationary ground, the axis of rotation would constantly shift as the missile maneuvered, making the equations unmanageable. Locking the description to the body frame keeps the axes fixed.

The _hat map_ $\hat{\cdot}$ turns a 3-vector into a $3\times3$ skew-symmetric matrix, and it exists for one reason only: (1c) needs to combine $\Omega$ with $R$, and you can't directly multiply a matrix by a cross product. The hat map re-packages "cross with $\Omega$" as an ordinary matrix, satisfying $\hat{\Omega}v = \Omega \times v$ for any $v$, so the equation can be written as a clean matrix product instead of a special case. A matrix $A$ is _skew-symmetric_ if $A^\top = -A$, which forces the diagonal entries to be zero and the off-diagonal entries to come in mirrored, sign-flipped pairs — leaving exactly three independent numbers, the same count as the vector $\Omega$ it came from. That's not a coincidence; it's why the conversion is lossless.

$I$ is the inertia matrix, encoding how hard the missile's mass is to spin about each of its own axes. Because of the dart-like shape, it's easy to roll about the long axis but much harder to tumble end-over-end about the short ones. Multiplying by $\Omega$ gives $I\Omega$, the angular momentum — how much rotation is stored up, in both size and direction. Because $I$ is a matrix rather than a scalar, $I\Omega$ doesn't generally point the same way $\Omega$ does: spin the missile about an axis it resists rotating about, and the momentum tilts away from the spin axis to compensate. $\dot{\Omega}$ is the angular acceleration, so $I\dot{\Omega}$, the left-hand side of (1d), is the rate of change of angular momentum — the rotational twin of $F = ma$.

The first term on the right of (1d), $I\Omega \times \Omega$, doesn't have an obvious parallel in the linear-dynamics case. It shows up because (1d) is written in the body frame, and the body frame is itself rotating — even with zero applied torque, angular momentum can appear to change when measured from a spinning frame, the same way the Coriolis effect shows up for anything measured in a rotating frame on Earth. This term is what makes an object tumble unpredictably when spun about an axis that isn't one of its principal axes — the same physics behind a thrown tennis racket or a flipped phone twisting mid-air instead of spinning cleanly. It's not something the controller adds; it's a property of rotating bodies that the controller has to account for.

$D(\Omega)$ is rotational drag: aerodynamic friction acting against the spin. The condition $\langle D(\Omega), \Omega \rangle \leq 0$ is a formal guarantee of passivity — this term only ever brakes the spin, never feeds energy into it.

$u$ is the applied torque, produced by deflecting the fins. It's the single control input in the entire rotational system — the only knob the autopilot actually gets to turn.

The chain of dependencies runs one way: orientation drives lift, lift drives velocity, velocity drives position. That cascade is the entire reason this problem needs two loops instead of one.

### Path following, not trajectory tracking

Most missile control literature is about chasing a target. This paper is about following a geometric path instead — a specific line segment $\ell$ in the inertial frame, defined by two waypoints $p_0, p_1 \in \mathbb{R}^3$.

The distinction matters for a reason the paper names directly: a gliding missile cannot control its own speed. Trajectory tracking asks the vehicle to be at a specific position at a specific time. If it falls behind, it has to speed up to catch the reference point — but there is no thrust to speed up with. The tracking paradigm is simply the wrong framework.

Path following asks only that the center of mass converge onto the line $\ell$ and stay there. _When_ along the line is irrelevant. The collection of all trajectories whose position lies on $\ell$ is called the _path following manifold_, and the control objective is to make this manifold a stable, attractive set — a set stabilization problem, not a point stabilization problem. This is the framework introduced in [Nielsen, Fulford, and Maggiore, 2010] (ref. [21] in the paper) and adapted here.

## The architecture: two loops, two time scales

The system in (1) has a cascade structure and the paper leans on it directly. Orientation changes fast — a torque can reorient the body in a fraction of a second. Position changes slow — it takes much longer for that reorientation to visibly bend the flight path. Figure 3 in the paper shows the architecture this earns: two controllers, running at two speeds, handing off through a single signal.

The _inner loop_ (fast) treats orientation as the thing to control directly. Given a desired orientation $R_d$, it applies torque $u$ to drive $(R, \Omega) \to (R_d, 0)$. It doesn't know or care about position or path — it just points the missile where it's told, as quickly as possible.

The _outer loop_ (slow) handles position. It designs a control law for the simplified system:

$$
\dot{r} = v \qquad \dot{v} = g\,i_z + w_1 v_y + w_2 v_z \tag{3}
$$

where $w_1$ and $w_2$ are fictitious direct inputs to the lift plane — as though a sideways thruster existed. No such thruster exists, of course. But the real lift equation (1b) is nonlinear and depends on orientation in a complicated way; solving it directly while simultaneously figuring out how to steer would be a mess. So the outer loop sidesteps that: design the controller as if we could dial in sideways acceleration through two clean knobs, then use an _orientation extraction_ step to translate whatever $(w_1, w_2)$ the outer loop asks for back into a real orientation $R_d$ — one that, if achieved, produces exactly that sideways push through lift. The pretending is temporary; by the time anything is commanded to the fins, it's gone.

This two-loop trick only works if the inner loop settles well before the outer loop has meaningfully progressed — otherwise treating them as separate design problems would be wrong. The paper validates this empirically in Figure 5: the inner loop's settling time is around $0.1$ (normalised units), the outer loop's around $1$ — an order of magnitude apart, which is enough to treat them as decoupled.

## The inner loop: stabilising an orientation

The inner loop's job is singular: given a desired orientation $R_d$, apply torque so the missile's actual orientation $R$ catches up to it and any leftover spin dies out, i.e. $(R, \Omega) \to (R_d, 0)$.

Before getting into the control law itself, it's worth asking why we represent orientation as a $3\times3$ matrix rather than three simple angles — roll, pitch, yaw. The two obvious alternatives both break down exactly where this missile needs to operate. Euler angles suffer from _gimbal lock_: at certain orientations, two of the three rotation axes line up and a degree of freedom effectively disappears from the math, even though the physical missile still has it. Quaternions avoid that, but carry a different problem: every orientation corresponds to two quaternions, $q$ and $-q$, representing the exact same physical rotation. A controller that isn't careful about this can get confused into unwinding — rotating 359 degrees to fix what was a 1-degree error. Working directly on $SO(3)$ sidesteps both. The cost is that "how far apart are two orientations" can no longer be computed by simple subtraction — it requires matrix operations, and that's most of what's going on in equation (5).

### The control law

The torque the paper applies is:

$$
u = -k_p \left(
\frac{
(R_d^\top R - R^\top R_d)^\vee
}{
\sqrt{1 + \operatorname{tr}(R^\top R_d)}
}
\right)
- k_d I\Omega,
\qquad
k_p, k_d > 0
\tag{5}
$$

At the right level of abstraction, this is just a PD controller written for rotations instead of plain numbers.

The second term, $-k_d I\Omega$, is the derivative part, it's proportional to current angular velocity and opposes it directly, the same role a derivative term plays in any PD controller.

The first term is the proportional part, i.e. the orientation error. $R^\top R_d$ is the rotation that is to take the body's current orientation to the desired one. When $R = R_d$ exactly, $R^\top R_d$ becomes the identity matrix and there's no error. When there's a mismatch, the numerator isolates the antisymmetric part of that mismatch via $R_d^\top R - R^\top R_d$, and the $(\cdot)^\vee$ map converts the resulting skew-symmetric matrix back into an ordinary 3-vector — an axis pointing in the direction the missile needs to rotate to close the gap, scaled by how far off it is. So the numerator is expressed in such a manner that $SO(3)$ needs instead of plain subtraction.

The denominator, $\sqrt{1 + \operatorname{tr}(R^\top R_d)}$, is a normalisation that rescales how hard the controller pushes as a function of how aligned $R$ and $R_d$ already are. I'll be honest — exactly why this particular form is the right one is tied into the structure of the stability proof in a way I'm not going to pretend to reconstruct here. What it does is keep the proportional term well-behaved across the full range of possible misalignments, including the awkward case where the missile is nearly 180 degrees from where it should be pointing.

### Why it works

To prove this control law actually drives $(R, \Omega)$ to $(R_d, 0)$, the paper constructs a Lyapunov function — a scalar that behaves like total energy for the system, which the proof shows can only ever decrease under (5), never increase:

$$
V(R, \Omega)
=
\frac{k_p}{2}\,
\operatorname{tr}\!\left[
(R^\top R_d - I_{3\times3})^\top
(R^\top R_d - I_{3\times3})
\right]
+
\sqrt{1+\operatorname{tr}(R^\top R_d)}
\;
\Omega^\top I\Omega
\tag{6}
$$

The first term measures how far $R$ is from $R_d$. When $R = R_d$, it's zero else it's positive and it acts as potential energy. The second term is rotational kinetic energy, $\Omega^\top I \Omega$, scaled by the same square-root factor from the denominator of (5), zero exactly when $\Omega = 0$.

By applying the control law (Equation 5) to the rotational dynamics, the authors mathematically guarantee that the time-derivative of the energy, $\dot{V}$, is strictly negative (or zero).

Because $SO(3)$ is a curved space, there's a risk the missile could get "stuck" balancing precisely 180 degrees away from the target (like a pendulum balanced upside down). To prove this won't happen, the authors use Rodrigues' rotation formula to show that the only place where the energy stops draining entirely (the only invariant set where $\dot{V} = 0$) is exactly at the target state $(R_d, 0)$. This guarantees the missile will asymptotically lock onto its assigned orientation.
