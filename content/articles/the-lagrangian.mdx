## Part One — The Foundations

### Work

Starting with something concrete. Say we push a box across a floor, some work is done. What does that mean, precisely?

Work is the energy transferred to an object by a force acting over a displacement. If we push with force $\vec{F}$ and the object moves by $d\vec{s}$, the work done is:

$$dW = \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{s}$$

The dot product is important. Both Force and displacement are vectors. The work done depends on the component of force that is parallel to the displacement. If we push at an angle, only the part of our push that is in the direction of motion does work. If the surface pushes up on the box while the box moves horizontally, the surface does no work — the force and the displacement are perpendicular, so $\vec{F} \cdot d\vec{s} = 0$.

For a force that varies along a path (which is the general case — gravity changes with height, springs change with extension), you integrate:

$$W_{12} = \int_1^2 \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{s}$$

This is the total work done in moving from point 1 to point 2 along some path.

### From Work to Potential Energy

Certain forces are path-independent, the work they do only depends on the start and end points, not the path taken. Say you carry a mass to a certain height. The work done against gravity is the same whether you take a straight line or a spiral. Springs and electrostatic forces behave the same way.

These are called _conservative_ forces.

If the work integral is path-independent, it can only depend on where you started and where you ended. So it must equal the change in some scalar function of position alone. That function is $-U$:

$$W_{12} = -\Delta U = U_1 - U_2$$

As for why it is negative? Let's firstly look at Hooke's law for a spring: $F = -kx$. If you stretch a spring and let go, it snaps back in an opposite direction hence we see that the force opposes the displacement, similarly for gravity, if you lift an object up, gravity pulls it down. The work done by these forces is negative because they resist the displacement. The potential energy $U$ increases as you stretch the spring or lift the object, so the work done by the force is negative of that increase.

In general, forces point towards lower energy — gravity pulls down because that's the direction potential energy decreases. In differential form:

$$\vec{F} \cdot d\vec{s} = -dU \implies \vec{F} = -\nabla U$$

Non-conservative forces like friction don't have this property — the work depends on path, energy bleeds into heat, and no scalar $U$ exists for them. The Lagrangian framework, at this stage, handles conservative forces only.

### Normal Force

The textbook version "whatever force the surface needs to exert to prevent penetration" describes the outcome, it leaves much wanting.

What actually happens? As two surfaces approach, their electron clouds overlap. The Pauli exclusion principle resists this — electrons cannot share quantum states. This resistance is the normal force. It isn't just some abstract "force" that exists, it is a real physical phenomenon arising from quantum mechanics.

The geometrically important fact: this repulsion acts perpendicular to the surface. The surface can push but not pull, and it always pushes normal to itself. Hence the name.

<Callout type="insight">
  The normal force between a bead and a wire, a book and a table, your hand and
  a wall — same mechanism every time. Electron clouds resist overlap. The
  direction is always perpendicular to the contact surface.
</Callout>

### Virtual Displacement

This is where Lagrangian mechanics diverges from Newtonian mechanics.

A real displacement $d\vec{r}$ happens in time — it's where the particle actually goes in the next instant $dt$.

A _virtual_ displacement $\delta\vec{r}$ is different. If time is frozen you ask: if I nudged the particle infinitesimally in a direction that doesn't violate the constraints, what directions are available? Nothing actually moves. No time passes. It's a thought experiment.

For a bead threaded on a wire, the virtual displacement must point along the wire — that's the only motion the constraint allows. The normal force from the wire points perpendicular to it. So:

$$\vec{F}_{\text{constraint}} \cdot \delta\vec{r} = 0$$

Constraint forces do no virtual work. Remember that the dot product ensures that only the component of the force in the direction of the virtual displacement contributes to the work. The bead is constrained to move along the wire, so its virtual displacement is tangent to the wire. The wire’s force on the bead is perpendicular to the wire, meaning it is always perpendicular to the allowed motion. Perpendicular vectors have zero dot product, so $$\vec{F}_{\text{constraint}} \cdot \delta\vec{r} = 0$$. Because of this, constraint forces do not contribute to the work and effectively drop out of the analysis.

The key idea is: the bead can only move along the wire, and the wire can only push normal to itself. Since allowed motion is tangent and force is normal at every point, there is no component of force in the direction of motion, so no work is done.

This is why we care so much about the Lagrangian formulation. In Newtonian mechanics, we have to know the normal force to write down $F = ma$ for the bead. In Lagrangian mechanics, we never have to know it. The constraint forces vanish from the equations of motion because they do no virtual work.

### D'Alembert's Principle

Newton's second law for particle $k$:

$$\vec{F}_k = m_k \ddot{\vec{q}}_k$$

Rearrange:

$$\vec{F}_k - m_k \ddot{\vec{q}}_k = 0$$

Take the dot product with virtual displacement $\delta\vec{q}_k$ and sum over all particles:

$$\sum_{k=1}^n (\vec{F}_k - m_k \ddot{\vec{q}}_k) \cdot \delta\vec{q}_k = 0$$

Now split $\vec{F}_k$ into applied forces (gravity, springs, whatever is actually driving the system) and constraint forces. The constraint forces vanish because $\vec{F}_{\text{constraint}} \cdot \delta\vec{q}_k = 0$. What remains:

$$\sum_{k=1}^n (\vec{F}_k^{\text{applied}} - m_k \ddot{\vec{q}}_k) \cdot \delta\vec{q}_k = 0$$

This is D'Alembert's principle. The constraint forces are gone and the equation involves only the forces we actually care about and the accelerations.

### Generalised Coordinates

One more tool before the main event. For a single particle in 3D we'd need three coordinates. For ten unconstrained particles we need thirty. But most systems have constraints: a pendulum bob is fixed to a rod, a bead is on a wire and planets move under central force.

Constraints reduce the number of independent variables. A pendulum bob in 2D might seem to need two coordinates $(x, y)$, but the rod constrains it to a circle — one coordinate, $\theta$, is enough. A double pendulum needs two angles.

The number of independent coordinates needed to fully specify a system's configuration is its _degrees of freedom_. We label these coordinates $q_1, q_2, \ldots, q_n$ and call them generalised coordinates. They don't have to be lengths — $\theta$ is a perfectly good generalised coordinate.

The payoff: when we express everything in generalised coordinates, the constraints are automatically satisfied. We never have to think about them again.

## Part Two — Hamilton's Principle

This is where the Lagrangian comes from.

### From D'Alembert to $\delta(K - U)$

Start from D'Alembert's principle:

$$\sum_{k=1}^n \vec{F}_k \cdot \delta\vec{q}_k = \sum_{k=1}^n m_k \ddot{\vec{q}}_k \cdot \delta\vec{q}_k$$

The left side. Since our forces are conservative, $\vec{F}_k = -\nabla_k U$. The work done by applied forces under a virtual displacement is $-\delta U$:

$$\sum_{k=1}^n \vec{F}_k \cdot \delta\vec{q}_k = -\delta U$$

The right side needs more work. The trick is to notice that:

$$\frac{d}{dt}(\dot{\vec{q}}_k \cdot \delta\vec{q}_k) = \ddot{\vec{q}}_k \cdot \delta\vec{q}_k + \dot{\vec{q}}_k \cdot \delta\dot{\vec{q}}_k$$

Solving for the first term:

$$\ddot{\vec{q}}_k \cdot \delta\vec{q}_k = \frac{d}{dt}(\dot{\vec{q}}_k \cdot \delta\vec{q}_k) - \dot{\vec{q}}_k \cdot \delta\dot{\vec{q}}_k$$

Also notice that $\dot{\vec{q}}_k \cdot \delta\dot{\vec{q}}_k = \frac{1}{2}\delta(|\dot{\vec{q}}_k|^2)$, since the variation $\delta$ acts like a differential on scalar quantities. Summing over all particles with mass factors:

$$\sum_{k=1}^n m_k \ddot{\vec{q}}_k \cdot \delta\vec{q}_k = \frac{d}{dt}\sum_{k=1}^n m_k \dot{\vec{q}}_k \cdot \delta\vec{q}_k - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^n m_k \delta(|\dot{\vec{q}}_k|^2)$$

The second term on the right is $-\delta K$, where $K = \frac{1}{2}\sum_k m_k |\dot{\vec{q}}_k|^2$ is the total kinetic energy. So:

$$\sum_{k=1}^n m_k \ddot{\vec{q}}_k \cdot \delta\vec{q}_k = \frac{d}{dt}\sum_{k=1}^n m_k \dot{\vec{q}}_k \cdot \delta\vec{q}_k + \delta K$$

Putting the two sides together:

$$-\delta U = \frac{d}{dt}\sum_{k=1}^n m_k \dot{\vec{q}}_k \cdot \delta\vec{q}_k + \delta K$$

Rearranging:

$$\delta(K - U) = \frac{d}{dt}\sum_{k=1}^n m_k \dot{\vec{q}}_k \cdot \delta\vec{q}_k$$

The left side is the variation of the difference between kinetic and potential energy. The right side is a pure total time derivative.

### Integrating — and Why the Boundary Term Vanishes

Integrate both sides from $t_1$ to $t_2$:

$$\delta\int_{t_1}^{t_2}(K - U)\,dt = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{k=1}^n m_k \dot{\vec{q}}_k \cdot \delta\vec{q}_k\,dt$$

The right side is the integral of a total time derivative — by the fundamental theorem of calculus, it just evaluates at the endpoints:

$$= \sum_{k=1}^n m_k \dot{\vec{q}}_k \cdot \delta\vec{q}_k\,\Bigg|_{t_1}^{t_2}$$

Now we impose the boundary condition: $\delta\vec{q}_k = 0$ at $t_1$ and $t_2$. _Why?_ Because we're asking which path — out of all paths connecting two fixed points in time — is the physical one. The start and end are fixed. We're only varying the middle. So the virtual displacement at the endpoints is zero, and the boundary term vanishes:

$$\delta\int_{t_1}^{t_2}(K - U)\,dt = 0$$

This is Hamilton's principle, derived.

<Callout type="insight">
  The boundary condition $\delta q = 0$ at the endpoints is not a trick. It's a
  statement about what question we're asking: given where the system is at $t_1$
  and where it is at $t_2$, which path does it take? The endpoints are not up
  for variation. Only the trajectory between them is.
</Callout>

### What is the Action?

Virtual displacement froze time and asked: given the constraints, what directions can the system move? The answer eliminated constraint forces from the problem.

Now unfreeze time. We have a system that starts at some configuration at $t_1$ and ends at another at $t_2$. Many paths through time connect those two points. The question is: which path does the system actually take?

D'Alembert's condition has to hold at every instant along that path — not just at one frozen moment. The natural thing to do is accumulate it over time. That's precisely what we did when we integrated both sides of:

$$\delta(K - U) = \frac{d}{dt}\sum_{k=1}^n m_k \dot{\vec{q}}_k \cdot \delta\vec{q}_k$$

The integral accumulated D'Alembert's condition across the whole trajectory. When the boundary terms vanished, what remained was $\delta\int(K-U),dt = 0$. This isn't a new principle imposed on top of the mechanics. It is D'Alembert's principle, summed over time.

We give $\int_{t_1}^{t_2} L,dt$ a name _the action, $S$_ because it is the single number that characterises a whole trajectory. At each instant, $L = K - U$ measures how the system is trading kinetic and potential energy. The action accumulates that measure over the entire path.

The physical trajectory is the one where the action is stationary: nudging any part of the path leaves $S$ unchanged to first order. Not minimum — stationary. The same sense in which a function has a stationary point where its derivative is zero, whether that point is a maximum, minimum, or saddle.

_What does first order mean here?_

$L = K - U = \frac{1}{2}m|\dot{q}|^2 - U(q)$ depends on two things at each instant: where the system is ($q$) and how fast it's moving ($\dot{q}$). To evaluate $L$ you need both. That's why we write $L(q, \dot{q})$.

When we nudge the path $q(t) \to q(t) + \delta q(t)$, the velocity changes too — velocity is just the time derivative of position, so:

$$\frac{d}{dt}[q + \delta q] = \dot{q} + \delta\dot{q}$$

Both arguments of $L$ are perturbed. Expanding $L$ at the nudged path is then just the multivariable Taylor expansion — for any function of two variables $f(x,y)$, a small perturbation gives $f(x+\varepsilon_1, y+\varepsilon_2) \approx f(x,y) + \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\varepsilon_1 + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\varepsilon_2$. The same applies here:

$$L(q + \delta q,\; \dot{q} + \delta\dot{q}) = L(q, \dot{q}) + \frac{\partial L}{\partial q}\delta q + \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}}\delta\dot{q} + \mathcal{O}(\delta q^2)$$

The leading-order terms are the two partial derivative pieces — linear in $\delta q$. The remainder involves $(\delta q)^2$ and higher. Since $\delta q$ is infinitesimal, $(\delta q)^2$ is incomparably smaller and is negligible in the same way $(dx)^2$ disappears in ordinary calculus. So the variation of the action is:

$$\delta S = S[q + \delta q] - S[q] = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \left[\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}\delta q + \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}}\delta\dot{q}\right] dt$$

Setting $\delta S = 0$ means this integral must vanish for _any_ choice of $\delta q$ — any nudge to the path, anywhere in time. That condition, that it holds for arbitrary $\delta q$, is what forces specific equations on $q(t)$. Extracting those equations is the next article.

### Why L = K − U Specifically

This is the question Goldstein doesn't answer satisfyingly. Let's be explicit.

We didn't choose $L = K - U$. It emerged. Look at the derivation: we started from Newton's second law and D'Alembert's principle. We manipulated the algebra. What appeared on the left side of the equation, naturally, was $\delta(K - U)$. That quantity integrated to zero because the boundary terms vanished.

So the Lagrangian $L = K - U$ is not a postulate about how to do mechanics differently. It is what falls out of Newton's laws when you ask about virtual displacements and conservative forces. The action principle is a repackaging of $F = ma$, not an alternative to it.

<Callout type="insight">
  This is why the Lagrangian is $K - U$ rather than $K + U$ (total energy).
  Total energy is conserved — it doesn't change along the path. The *difference*
  $K - U$ is what captures the dynamics, because it responds to the shape of the
  trajectory. The system trades kinetic and potential energy as it moves — their
  difference is the thing that, integrated over time, is stationary on the
  physical path.
</Callout>

## What We Have

Hamilton's principle in one line:

$$\delta\int_{t_1}^{t_2}L\,dt = 0 \qquad L = K - U$$

The physical path is the one that makes the action stationary. This is exact, not approximate. It applies to any conservative mechanical system. It requires no knowledge of constraint forces — they vanished with D'Alembert. And it arrived directly from $F = ma$.

What we don't yet have: the equations of motion. Hamilton's principle tells us that the physical path satisfies $\delta S = 0$ — but it doesn't yet tell us _what equations that path must satisfy_. Extracting those equations requires asking what $\delta S = 0$ actually implies about $L$ and $q(t)$. That calculation — the calculus of variations applied to this functional — is the Euler-Lagrange equation.
